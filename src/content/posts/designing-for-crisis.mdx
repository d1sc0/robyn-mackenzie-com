When someone lands on an emergency triage page or a critical support service, their cognitive bandwidth is severely constricted. Adrenaline narrows focus, stress increases reading errors, and complex multi-column layouts induce immediate cognitive fatigue.

> "Accessibility isn't just about screen reader compliance or high contrast ratios; it's about emotional accessibility when someone is having the worst day of their week."

### Principles for High-Stress Interfaces

Here are four core principles I carry from my work on healthcare transformation projects:

1. **One Question Per Screen:** Never overwhelm users with sprawling multi-fieldset forms when they are anxious.
2. **Plain English First:** Avoid institutional jargon. If you mean *“tell us about your pain”*, do not write *“describe symptomatic presentation”*.
3. **Persistent Reassurance:** Clarify that progress is saved and help is on the way.
4. **Predictable Navigation:** Never trap users behind unpredictable JavaScript modaled drawers.

### Code Snippet: Semantic Alert Patterns

```html
<!-- Accessible High-Priority Notice -->
<aside class="notice notice--urgent" role="status" aria-live="polite">
  <p class="notice__heading">Immediate Assistance Available</p>
  <p>If you or someone you are with needs urgent care, call <strong>111</strong> directly.</p>
</aside>
```

### Usability Checklist for Service Pages

- [x] Clear primary action visible without scrolling
- [x] Reading age tested at 9–11 years
- [x] No auto-advancing carousels or flashing motion
- [x] Tested with users under time pressure and mobile network latency

When we design for the edges of human vulnerability, the experience improves exponentially for everyone.
